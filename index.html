AI for Job Seekers

Workshop Reference

Everything you need to get started and keep going — before, during, and after the workshop. Replace anything in [brackets] with your own information.

Step 1

Create a free AI account

Sign up for any one of these — they're all free and work great for job searching. If you already have an account with any of them, you're all set.

Claude
claude.ai
Great for longer writing and thoughtful responses
ChatGPT
chatgpt.com
The most widely used — familiar to many employers
Gemini
gemini.google.com
Works seamlessly with Google Docs & Gmail
Perplexity
perplexity.ai
Good for researching companies and job types
Not sure which one to pick? Any of them will work for what we're doing. Pick whichever name you recognize, or just go with Claude or ChatGPT.
Step 2

Write your work story

Open any document — Google Doc, Word, Notes app, whatever's easiest — and write a plain-language story about your work experience. This doesn't need to be polished or formal. Write it the way you'd tell a friend.

  • Jobs you've had — paid, unpaid, volunteer, caregiving, anything counts
  • What you actually did in each role
  • What you liked about those jobs
  • What you didn't like or wouldn't want to do again
  • Any skills or strengths you feel good about — even if you've never been paid for them
Don't love writing? Try talking instead.

Dictation.io lets you speak and it types your words in real time — no account, no download. Works in Spanish and many other languages. AI tools can read and respond in any language.

Once your story is written, save it somewhere easy to find. You'll copy and paste it into your AI tool to get started.
Prompts

Clarify your ideal job target

Start here. Use your work story to discover the job titles, industries, and companies that fit who you are.

Start here
"Here's what I've done. Help me spot skills and strengths — look for patterns that show up across multiple roles, including unpaid and informal work."
Paste or attach your work story document.
Follow-up
"What are some job titles that fit my skills and strengths? Give me a list of 5–8 job titles with a one-sentence explanation of why each fits."
Follow-up
"Which industries need people with those job titles? List the top 5 industries, ranked by how many job openings typically exist."
Follow-up
"What types of companies hire people with those job titles? Give me 3–5 specific types of companies, with one example of a real company for each type."
Follow-up
"Based on the job titles and industries we just identified, which of them are most likely to remain in demand as AI takes over more tasks? For each one, tell me specifically why — what does this role involve that AI handles poorly or not at all?"
Prompts

Set up your job search documents

The more context you give an AI tool about yourself, the more useful its answers will be. Building a small library of documents you can paste from saves you time in every future conversation.

Prompt
"I'm starting a job search. List the 5 most useful documents I should create to make it easier to use AI tools throughout my search. For each one, tell me what to include and why it helps."
Prompts

Find, understand, and respond to job listings

Finding jobs

Prompt
"What are some keywords I should search for if I am looking for work as a [your ideal job description]? Give me 10–15 keywords or job title variations, grouped by: exact titles, related titles, and industry terms that show up in listings."

Understanding a listing

Prompt
"Here's a job listing — can you explain what this role actually does? Give me a plain-English summary in 3–5 sentences, then a short list of the 3 things this job will mostly involve day-to-day."
Paste the full job listing.
Prompt
"Does this job sound like a good fit for someone with my background? Be direct. Tell me the 2–3 strongest matches and the 1–2 biggest gaps I'd need to address."
Paste the job listing and your resume.

Tailoring your resume

Prompt
"Here's the job I'm applying for and my resume draft. Can you help make it a better match?"
Paste the job listing and your resume.

Writing a cover letter

Prompt
"Here's the job I'm applying for and my resume. I've also attached the hiring manager's LinkedIn profile. Draft a cover letter that leads with the business problem this role is probably solving, and positions me as someone who can solve it. Don't open with 'I am writing to apply' or list my credentials in the first paragraph."
Paste the job listing and your resume. Attach the hiring manager's LinkedIn profile as a PDF if you can find it.

Reaching out directly

Prompt
"I am sending a cold email to the hiring manager for this job. Draft a professional but friendly email message. Keep it under 150 words. Lead with one specific reason I'm reaching out to them in particular."
Paste the job listing, your resume, your cover letter, and the hiring manager's LinkedIn profile.
Prompts

Interview prep

Prompt
"I got an interview for this job! I'm really nervous. Can you write some interview questions so I can practice? Focus on questions specific to this role and company, not generic interview questions I could find anywhere. Include at least two behavioral questions based on the job requirements."
Paste the job listing, your resume, your cover letter, the hiring manager's LinkedIn profile, and any messages you've exchanged.
Prompts

Networking

Step 1 — Uncover connections

Find out who you should be talking to. Goal: a list of at least 25 people to connect with.

Prompt — groups you belong to
"Here are groups I'm part of. What types of people in these groups are most likely to be connected to [your target — e.g. hiring managers in healthcare, small business owners, HR professionals]?"
Paste your list of groups — church, volunteer org, sports club, professional group, parent group, hobby group, etc.
Follow-up
"Which of these groups should I prioritize, and why?"
Prompt — your work history
"Here are companies I've worked at. What types of people from these workplaces are most likely to be connected to [your target]?"
Paste your list of past employers or volunteer organizations. Former colleagues are often your warmest leads.
Prompt — your existing contacts
"Here are people in my network: [paste list of names and job titles]. Which of them are most likely to know about openings for someone with my background, and which are most likely to know someone who does? Keep your response brief — just a short list with a one-sentence reason for each."
Then find specific names. Go to the websites and LinkedIn pages for each group. List the names of people you already know first. Then look for names you don't recognize — board members, event organizers, committee chairs — and add them too.

Step 2 — Feed curation

Turn your LinkedIn feed into a focused professional tool. Goal: 25–49 people you're actively paying attention to.

Remember: unfollowing is not disconnecting. You stay connected and can still message them — their posts just stop appearing in your feed.
Prompt — find who to unfollow
"Here are recent posts from my LinkedIn feed. Based on the content, are there any posters I should unfollow to keep my feed focused on [your industry / target companies / job function]?"
Paste ~20 posts copied from your LinkedIn feed. Copy post text directly — no need to format it.
Prompt — advanced, if you have 200+ connections
"These are my LinkedIn connections. What patterns do you see in terms of industries, seniority levels, and gaps that might be relevant to a job search in [field]?"
Attach a .csv of your LinkedIn connections. To download: LinkedIn → Settings → Data Privacy → Get a copy of your data → Connections.
Follow-up
"I want to prioritize contacts located in [your region, e.g. the Portland Metro area] who work as [job title] in [industry]. Identify the contacts who do NOT fit these criteria."
Use the output to unfollow low-priority contacts in bulk.

Garden networking

For when you're not urgently job searching. Do this consistently — daily, weekly, or monthly — to stay visible and build relationships over time.

Prompt — find posts worth commenting on
"Of these posts, which ones would be worth commenting on if I want to stay visible to people in [my field / my target industry]? For each one, suggest an angle for a genuinely useful comment."
Paste ~20 posts from your LinkedIn feed. You can reuse the same posts you pasted for feed curation.
Pro tip for posts with lots of comments. On a post with hundreds of reactions, your comment may get lost. Try replying to a specific commenter instead — you'll be much more visible to that person. Paste the post AND its comments into AI and ask: "Are any of these comments worth replying to?"
Prompt — draft a comment
"Here's a LinkedIn post from [a hiring manager at a company I'd like to work for / a contact I want to stay connected with]. I want to leave a comment that adds value and sounds like me — not generic, not sycophantic. Here's a little about my background: [paste 2–3 sentences about yourself]. Draft two or three options."
Always edit before posting. The goal is for it to sound like you — because in the final version, it is you.

Hunter networking

For when you need a job now. Do this every weekday during a dedicated time block — focused, direct outreach to specific people.

Prompt — find who to reach out to
"I am seeking work as a [job title] in [industry/location]. Based on my background, who are the types of people most likely to either refer me directly to a job opening or connect me with someone who can?"
Paste 3–4 sentences describing your most relevant experience, your strongest skills, and the type of work environment you do best in.
Prompt — research a contact before reaching out
"Here is the LinkedIn profile of someone I want to reach out to. What do we have in common, and what's the most genuine connection point I could reference in a message?"
Paste their LinkedIn profile text.
Prompt — reconnecting after a gap
"Here is what I know about [name]: [paste what you know]. I want to reconnect after [timeframe, e.g. two years]. What's a natural, non-transactional reason to reach out right now?"
Natural reconnection feels like interest in them — not a request.

Writing outreach messages

Prompt — LinkedIn DM
"I want to send a LinkedIn DM to [name/job title] asking if they know of any openings for someone with my background. I don't want it to sound desperate or transactional. Here's my background: [paste]. Draft two options — one warmer, one more direct."
Pick the option that sounds most like you, then edit before sending.
Prompt — warm intro follow-up email
"I've been referred to [name] by [mutual contact]. Draft a short email introducing myself and asking for a 20-minute call. Here's my background: [paste]. Here's what I know about them: [paste]."
Mention the mutual contact in the first sentence — it's your best credibility signal.
Prompt — asking for a warm introduction
"I want to ask [name] to introduce me to [target contact]. We know each other from [context]. Draft a short, easy-to-forward message that makes it simple for them to say yes."
Make it easy — include a one-sentence description of yourself they can use in the intro.

When outreach isn't working

Prompt
"I've reached out to [number] people in the past [timeframe] and haven't heard back. Here's an example of a message I've been sending: [paste]. Give me your rewrite first, then a brief explanation of what you changed and why."
Not hearing back is feedback. Use AI to diagnose before sending more.
Always give AI your context. The more specific you are, the more useful the output. Before any networking prompt, include: your background, your target, and your relationship to the person you're reaching out to.
